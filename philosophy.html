Design Philosophy

Three ideas I keep coming back to, in design and in everything else I make: empathy, uniqueness, and judgment.

Empathy

Every interface gets tired of being beautiful. Give someone enough time with even the most polished screen, and the polish stops registering. What keeps a product alive past the hundredth use isn’t its color palette or its motion curves. It’s whether the person who built it actually understood the person using it.

The inventions that last are usually acts of love pushed far enough to become useful. Surgical gloves exist because a surgeon couldn’t keep watching someone’s hands suffer for his work. Tenji blocks, the raised tiles under your feet at train platforms and crossings across Japan and now most of the world, exist because one man cared enough about a friend who couldn’t see to carve a warning into the ground. Nobody assigned either of them a brief. They loved someone specific, past the point of sympathy, and what came out the other side was an invention.

Björk said something once, back when everyone insisted electronic music had no soul: if there’s no soul in the music, it’s because nobody put it there. You don’t get to blame the machine. I think about that every time someone says the same thing about AI and design. AI can’t put a soul into anything, it was never going to. It hasn’t loved anyone enough to notice their hands hurting, or their next step going unmarked. That part was never the computer’s job. It’s still ours.

Uniqueness

The problems worth solving usually start with someone you actually know, struggling with something specific. Not a persona, not a user segment. A dashboard for people living with Alzheimer’s doesn’t come from market research. It comes from watching someone you love lose track of their own day, and wanting to hand a little of it back.

This is why UI and UX matter more than almost anything else in a product. Nobody reads the manual. People just use the thing in front of them, and what they understand of it in the first ten seconds is the whole pitch. The interface isn’t decoration on top of the product. Most of the time, it is the product.

When something you designed finally ships, people sometimes ask who made it, and the honest answer usually points at whoever wrote the code. I’ve stopped minding that. Unlike a lot of designers I know, I don’t need my name in the credits to know what’s mine. I was there before a single component existed. The idea, the shape of the problem, the decision about what the person on the other end should feel first: that begins with the designer, whether or not anyone in the room remembers to say so.

Humans build families, and companies, and cities. But design, the real part of it, isn’t quite a human thing. It’s closer to nature. Something gets decided before it’s fully understood, and then it exists, and the world rearranges itself slightly around it. Call that God, if you believe in one. Call it nature, if you don’t. Either way, once you’ve made something like that, you don’t need anyone in the room to confirm it belongs to you. You already know.

Judgment

AI democratizes execution. It does not democratize expertise. Someone can now prompt a model to produce an attractive interface without understanding information architecture, interaction design, cognitive load, accessibility, hierarchy, mental models, responsive behavior, or why one solution is preferable to another. What comes out might look like a finished screen. It isn’t a finished decision.

Giving someone a model and calling them a designer is giving someone Photoshop and calling them an art director. The software was never the hard part. The tool makes production accessible. It has never once conferred judgment, and judgment was always the actual job. If anything, it’s become the one part of the job a machine still can’t touch.

This is where I think the field is actually headed. When AI can build almost anything, someone still has to decide what should get built and what shouldn’t. I think that decision belongs to us, the people trained to sit with a user’s confusion until it turns into a clear path, not to whoever wrote the model. AI is only data. It can only look backward, at what has already been made, already been said, already been felt. Creativity looks forward, toward what hasn’t existed yet. Empathy is further out of reach for it than creativity even is. It isn’t a pattern you can train on. It’s noticing that someone’s hands hurt before they’ve said a word about it.

I say this to myself more than to anyone else: my latest work will always be my best work. Not because I’m precious about whatever I shipped last, but because I intend to keep getting closer to the thing only a person can actually make.

This is the thinking behind the work.
